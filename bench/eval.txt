How a City Drinks

Most people never think about where their water comes from until the day it stops. A tap is turned, water arrives, and the question of how it got there feels about as interesting as the question of why the floor holds us up. Yet the system behind that tap is one of the largest machines a city ever builds, and almost all of it is hidden underground.

The story usually begins far outside the city limits. A river, a lake or a deep layer of porous rock holds water that fell as rain months or years earlier. Engineers choose a source by asking three questions: how much water can be taken without harming the source, how clean it is to begin with, and how much energy it will take to move it. A mountain reservoir may be expensive to build, but gravity then does most of the work of carrying water downhill for free. A river next to the city is cheap to reach, but its water may carry everything that farms and towns upstream have washed into it.

Once water is drawn in, it goes to a treatment plant. The first steps are almost mechanical. Screens catch branches, leaves and the occasional fish. Then chemicals called coagulants are mixed in. They cause tiny particles of clay and organic matter, which would otherwise stay suspended forever, to clump together into larger flakes. In slow, quiet basins those flakes settle to the bottom, and the clearer water above flows on to filters made of layers of sand and gravel. By the time it leaves the filters, the water looks clean, but looking clean is not the same as being safe.

Disinfection is the step that changed public health more than almost any medicine. Before cities treated their water, diseases such as cholera and typhoid moved through crowded neighbourhoods in waves. Adding a small, carefully measured amount of chlorine kills most bacteria and viruses, and a little of it stays in the water as it travels through the pipes, protecting it on the way. Some plants also use ultraviolet light or ozone, which work quickly and leave no taste, though they offer no protection once the water has left the plant.

Then comes the hardest part to see: distribution. A city of a million people may have thousands of kilometres of pipe beneath its streets, some of it more than a century old. Water has to reach the top floor of a tall building and the farthest house on the edge of town at a useful pressure, all day and all night, while demand rises every morning when people wake up and every evening when they cook. Pumps push water into tall towers and hilltop tanks, and those tanks act like batteries. They fill when demand is low and drain when it is high, so the pumps do not have to chase every change minute by minute.

Leaks are the quiet enemy of the whole system. In many cities, a fifth or more of the treated water never reaches a customer. It seeps out of cracked joints and corroded mains, often without ever appearing at the surface. Finding those leaks is a strange kind of detective work. Crews walk the streets at night with listening devices, because a leak makes a faint hiss that is easier to hear when traffic is gone. Newer systems place sensors along the pipes and compare pressure readings, looking for the small drop that means water is escaping somewhere between two points.

After it is used, water does not disappear. It leaves homes through a second network of pipes and flows, mostly by gravity, toward a wastewater plant. There, the process runs in reverse of what many people expect. Instead of relying mainly on chemicals, these plants rely on living things. Large tanks are filled with bacteria that feed on the organic waste, and air is pumped through them so the bacteria can breathe. The bacteria multiply, eat, and clump together, and the clumps are allowed to settle out. What remains is clean enough to return to a river, and in some dry regions it is treated further and used again for parks, industry or even drinking water.

Every part of this system involves trade-offs. Bigger pipes cost more but waste less energy to friction. More storage makes the network resilient but lets water sit longer, and old water can lose its protective chlorine. Cheaper pumps may use more electricity over their lifetime than they saved when they were bought. The engineers who run a water system spend their careers balancing these choices against budgets that are always tight and customers who notice only when something goes wrong.

The next time you fill a glass, it is worth remembering that the water in it may have fallen as snow on a distant mountain, travelled through a tunnel longer than most highways, been stirred, settled, filtered and disinfected, and then pushed uphill by machines that never stop running. It is one of the quietest achievements of modern life, and it works best when nobody notices it at all.
